## GOAL 1: TIER 1 VALIDATION — INTERNAL CONSISTENCY CHECKS

This goal is about verifying that the trained Gradient Boosting models obey fundamental geochemical rules. The script tier1_validation_chemical_logic.py (located in python_scripts/) runs eight chemical logic tests against the training data: (1) higher pH should decrease percent free metal because hydroxide and carbonate complexation increases, (2) any chelator should reduce percent free metal compared to the no-chelator baseline, (3) higher chelator dose should monotonically decrease percent free metal, (4) more surface sites (Clay > Loam > Sand) should decrease percent free metal through sorption, (5) EDTA should outperform NTA for lead and copper because EDTA has higher thermodynamic stability constants with these metals, (6) zinc should be harder to chelate than copper across all conditions reflecting known stability constant differences, (7) chelator effectiveness should be greater at higher pH because hydrogen ions compete with metals for chelator binding sites at low pH, and (8) high ionic strength should reduce free lead and copper through chloride complexation, which is specific to Rhode Island coastal conditions. Each test uses tolerance thresholds to account for numerical noise (typically 0.5 to 3 percent depending on the rule). If any rule fails, it could indicate either a real problem in the PHREEQC simulations, an edge case where the rule does not strictly apply, or a threshold that needs adjustment. The output is a summary CSV (data/tier1_validation_report.csv) showing pass/fail status, pass rate, and example violations for each rule. Running this script requires only pandas and numpy and operates on the existing training data without needing the trained model files. This is the first and most immediate validation step and should be completed before any other validation work.

## GOAL 2: TIER 2 VALIDATION — LITERATURE BENCHMARKING

This goal is about comparing the model's predictions against published experimental results from peer-reviewed chelator-assisted soil remediation studies. The framework consists of two components: a CSV template (data/literature_benchmark_data.csv) that the researcher populates with data extracted from published papers, and a Python script (python_scripts/tier2_literature_benchmark.py) that maps published experimental conditions to the model's input features and compares predictions to observed outcomes. The CSV template has columns for citation information, soil properties (texture, pH, organic matter percent, CEC, electrical conductivity), metal concentrations in mg/kg, chelator type and dose with units, contact time, liquid-to-solid ratio, and observed extraction efficiency. The script includes mapping functions that convert published values to the model's categorical inputs: soil texture descriptions are mapped to Sand/Loam/Clay, organic matter percent is converted to DOC using the van Bemmelen factor (DOC = OM% x 10 x 0.58) and binned to 10/25/40 mg/L, chelator doses in various units (mmol/kg, g/L, mM) are converted to mg/L and mapped to 50/150/300, ionic strength is estimated from electrical conductivity or sodium concentrations, and moisture conditions are inferred from experimental descriptions. The comparison is necessarily approximate because literature studies report extraction efficiency (percent of metal removed from soil) while our model predicts percent free metal in pore water, and because batch slurry experiments do not perfectly replicate in-situ soil conditions. Therefore validation focuses on directional agreement (did the chelator help?), relative ranking (which chelator worked best?), and general magnitude consistency rather than exact numerical match. The target is 15 to 25 literature data points from journals such as Chemosphere, Journal of Hazardous Materials, Environmental Pollution, Science of the Total Environment, and Soil and Sediment Contamination. Priority should be given to studies that test EDTA, NTA, or citrate on lead, copper, zinc, or cadmium contaminated soils at pH values between 5 and 8, as these align best with our training data range.

## GOAL 3: TIER 3 VALIDATION — BENCH-SCALE LABORATORY EXPERIMENTS

This goal is the gold standard for validation and would provide the strongest evidence for a publication or commercial product. The plan is to collect 3 to 5 actual contaminated soil samples from Rhode Island sites (ideally representing different textures, contamination levels, and proximity to coast), characterize them fully (pH, total metals by ICP-MS or XRF, organic matter by loss-on-ignition, texture by hydrometer, CEC by ammonium acetate, electrical conductivity, and mineralogy if possible by XRD), and then run batch extraction experiments with EDTA, NTA, and citrate at multiple doses (matching the model's 50, 150, and 300 mg/L levels). Each experiment would use a controlled liquid-to-solid ratio, be run in triplicate, and measure dissolved metal concentrations in the extract by ICP-OES or ICP-MS at multiple time points (1, 4, 24, 48 hours) to capture kinetic effects. The measured dissolved metal speciation (ideally free ion activity measured by ion-selective electrode or Donnan membrane technique, or at minimum total dissolved concentration) would be compared to model predictions. This experimental program would generate 30 to 45 real data points and would constitute a thesis chapter or a standalone publication. The comparison between model predictions and experimental observations would reveal how well thermodynamic equilibrium predictions match real soil behavior, quantify the "reality gap" introduced by kinetic effects and biological processes, and identify conditions where the model is most and least reliable. This goal requires laboratory access, analytical instruments, and several weeks of experimental work, so it is a medium-term priority that should be planned in parallel with other development work.

## GOAL 4: MULTI-MODEL INTEGRATION — PHREEQC, ORCHESTRA, AND HP1/HPx

This goal addresses the fundamental limitations of using PHREEQC alone by integrating predictions from multiple geochemical and reactive transport models to create a more robust training dataset. PHREEQC provides thermodynamic equilibrium speciation in a closed batch system, which is a necessary starting point but does not capture transport, kinetics, or the open-system nature of real soils. ORCHESTRA is a flexible geochemical modeling framework developed at Wageningen University that excels at organic matter-metal interactions using the NICA-Donnan model (which would replace our current approximation of humic and fulvic acids as simple DOC additions) and can handle kinetic reactions with user-defined rate expressions. HP1 (or its successor HPx) couples HYDRUS-1D for variably saturated water flow with PHREEQC for reactive chemistry, enabling simulation of chelator transport through soil profiles with simultaneous speciation, sorption, and precipitation reactions — this directly addresses the multi-horizon question because it can model a chelator solution applied at the surface percolating through horizons with different properties. The integration strategy would be: generate the same parameter combinations in all three models, compare their predictions, and then train the ML model on the full ensemble of results. When models agree, we have high confidence. When they disagree, we have several options: (a) use the mean prediction as a consensus estimate, (b) weight models based on their validated accuracy against literature/bench data (Tier 2 and 3), (c) use the disagreement itself as a feature — the ML model could learn which conditions produce high inter-model variance and flag those predictions as less certain, or (d) use a Bayesian model averaging approach where each model's prediction is weighted by its posterior probability given the validation data. The practical approach is to start with PHREEQC (already done), add ORCHESTRA next for improved organic matter modeling, and add HP1 last for transport. Each addition expands the training data and improves the model's ability to represent real soil complexity. ORCHESTRA requires Java and can be scripted, and HP1 requires HYDRUS which has a separate license. Both can be run in batch mode similar to our current PHREEQC pipeline.

## GOAL 5: ADDING NEW METALS — MERCURY AND BEYOND

This goal expands the model from four metals (Pb, Cu, Zn, Cd) to include additional contaminants of concern, starting with mercury (Hg) and potentially expanding to chromium (Cr), nickel (Ni), and arsenic (As). Mercury is the highest priority addition because it is a major contaminant in many Rhode Island sites (legacy industrial and maritime activity) and it presents unique modeling challenges. Hg exists in multiple forms: Hg2+ (mercuric, behaves similarly to other divalent metals for chelation), Hg0 (elemental, volatile, not chelatable), and methylmercury CH3Hg+ (the most toxic form, produced by microbial methylation in anaerobic zones). PHREEQC with minteq.v4.dat does include Hg2+ speciation, HgCl2 complexes, and HgS precipitation, so generating Hg training data through the existing pipeline is feasible for inorganic mercury. However, the model cannot predict methylation because that is a biologically-mediated kinetic process controlled by sulfate-reducing bacteria and iron-reducing bacteria in anaerobic sediments. The strategy for Hg is: (1) add Hg2+ to PHREEQC simulations alongside the existing four metals, (2) add hg_percent_free as a fifth target variable, (3) include a methylation risk flag in the interface that warns users when site conditions (low pe, high sulfate, high organic matter) are favorable for methylmercury formation, and (4) clearly communicate to users that chelation addresses inorganic Hg but does not prevent or remediate methylmercury. For chromium, the key challenge is that Cr exists in two oxidation states with completely different behavior: Cr(III) is relatively immobile and non-toxic, while Cr(VI) is highly mobile, toxic, and carcinogenic. Chelation is relevant for Cr(III) mobilization but Cr(VI) requires reduction rather than chelation, so the model would need to handle both forms. Arsenic was previously excluded because it exists as an oxyanion (arsenate, arsenite) rather than a cation and is not effectively chelated by EDTA or citrate, but it could be included with a different remediation framework (iron-based amendments rather than chelators). Nickel is more straightforward as it behaves similarly to Zn and Cd with chelators. The phased approach is: add Hg first (next data generation round), then Ni (similar chemistry to existing metals, easy addition), then Cr (requires redox-dependent modeling), and consider As only if the tool expands beyond chelation to include other remediation amendments.

## GOAL 6: ADDING NEW CHELATORS AND COMBINATIONS

This goal expands the chelator options from the current five (EDTA, NTA, Citrate, Humic, Fulvic) to include newer and more environmentally sustainable chelating agents, and to model chelator combinations. Priority additions include: GLDA (glutamic acid diacetic acid, marketed as Dissolvine GL, readily biodegradable and increasingly favored by regulators), HIDS (hydroxyiminodisuccinic acid, another biodegradable alternative), EDDHA (ethylenediamine-N,N'-bis(2-hydroxyphenylacetic acid), very strong iron chelator also effective for other metals), and DTPA (revisit with better thermodynamic data than our initial attempt — DTPA is widely used in soil science and the initial poor performance was likely due to manual thermodynamic definition errors rather than true poor performance). For GLDA and HIDS, thermodynamic stability constants may not be in the minteq.v4.dat database, so we would need to add them manually using the PHASES or SOLUTION_SPECIES blocks in PHREEQC, sourcing stability constants from the NIST Critical Stability Constants database or from the primary literature (Kołodyńska 2011, Pinto et al. 2014). Chelator combinations are used in practice in several ways: sequential application (EDTA first for Pb/Cu, then citrate for residual Zn), simultaneous mixtures (EDTA plus citrate at various ratios), and chelator plus amendment combinations (chelator plus lime to raise pH, chelator plus biochar for residual sorption, chelator plus surfactant for hydrophobic co-contaminants). Modeling combinations in PHREEQC is straightforward — both chelators are added to the SOLUTION block and thermodynamic competition is handled automatically. The training data expansion would include chelator pairs at three ratios (75:25, 50:50, 25:75) for the major combinations (EDTA+Citrate, EDTA+NTA, NTA+Citrate), plus sequential application scenarios. This would add approximately 2,000 to 5,000 new training scenarios. The interface would need a "combination mode" where users can select multiple chelators and see predicted performance of the mixture versus individual agents.

## GOAL 7: MULTI-HORIZON MODE FOR THE INTERFACE

This goal addresses the real-world complexity that contamination and soil properties vary with depth across soil horizons. A contaminated site might have an A horizon (topsoil) that is sandy loam with high organic matter, pH 5.8, and the highest metal concentrations, underlain by a B horizon (subsoil) that is clay with lower organic matter, pH 6.5, more iron oxides, and moderate metal concentrations, above a C horizon (parent material) with different properties entirely. The current interface treats the soil as a single homogeneous layer. The multi-horizon mode would allow users to input parameters for up to three horizons (matching typical A/B/C horizon structure), with fields for: horizon name/depth, thickness, pH, texture (sand/silt/clay percentages with USDA classification), organic matter percent, metal concentrations, and moisture condition. The model would run predictions independently for each horizon and then provide an integrated recommendation. The integration logic would need to consider: which horizon has the highest contamination (usually A, so it often dominates the recommendation), whether the recommended chelator for the most contaminated horizon would cause problems in other horizons (for example, mobilizing metals that then re-sorb or precipitate in the B horizon — which could actually be beneficial), and whether the chelator can physically reach deeper horizons (which depends on soil permeability and application method). The initial implementation would run parallel independent predictions and present results side-by-side with a summary recommendation. A more sophisticated version (after HP1 integration from Goal 4) could model actual vertical transport of the chelator solution through the profile. The interface would include a visual soil profile diagram showing horizons with color-coded contamination levels and predicted chelator effectiveness at each depth.

## GOAL 8: PUBLISHABLE METHODOLOGY PAPER

This goal is to write and submit a peer-reviewed paper documenting the PHREEQC-to-ML pipeline as a novel approach for chelator selection in soil remediation. The paper would be structured as: (1) Introduction covering the problem of heavy metal contamination in urban and coastal soils, the challenge of chelator selection, and the gap between thermodynamic modeling and practical decision-making; (2) Methods describing the PHREEQC simulation design (parameter ranges, database selection, surface complexation setup, chelator modeling approach for EDTA/NTA/citrate/humic/fulvic), the experimental design for 12,636 training scenarios covering realistic Rhode Island conditions, the ML pipeline (feature engineering, Gradient Boosting training, hyperparameter tuning, cross-validation), and the three-tier validation framework; (3) Results presenting model performance metrics (R-squared, RMSE, cross-validated scores for all four metals), feature importance analysis (confirming pH as dominant variable, chelator type and dose as key treatment variables), internal consistency validation (chemical logic checks), and literature benchmarking comparison; (4) Discussion addressing model strengths (rapid screening across thousands of conditions, identification of optimal chelator-dose-pH combinations, applicability to site-specific conditions), limitations (thermodynamic equilibrium assumption, simplified organic matter modeling, no kinetic or biological processes, closed system assumption), and future directions (multi-model integration, bench validation, new metals and chelators); and (5) Practical implications showing how the Streamlit interface translates complex geochemistry into actionable recommendations for practitioners. Target journals in order of preference: Environmental Science and Technology (highest impact, best fit for methodology papers combining modeling and remediation), Journal of Hazardous Materials (strong remediation focus, likely receptive to decision-support tools), Chemosphere (broad environmental chemistry scope), or Science of the Total Environment (good for applied environmental tools). The paper should emphasize three novel contributions: (a) the systematic use of geochemical simulation to generate training data for ML rather than relying on sparse experimental data, (b) the multi-metal, multi-chelator comparison framework that goes beyond single-metal studies, and (c) the practical translation into a decision-support tool for remediation practitioners. Supplementary materials would include the complete training dataset, model code, and the Streamlit application code.

## GOAL 9: SCALING TO A COMMERCIAL PRODUCT

This goal covers the path from research prototype to a product that environmental consultants and remediation specialists would pay to use. The current Streamlit interface is appropriate for a prototype and for the publication, but a commercial product would need: (a) a proper web application with a React or similar frontend and a Python API backend serving model predictions, deployed on cloud infrastructure (AWS, GCP, or Azure) to handle multiple concurrent users; (b) user authentication and account management; (c) the ability to save and retrieve past analyses for a site; (d) exportable reports formatted for regulatory submissions (PDF reports with site parameters, predictions, confidence intervals, and chelator recommendations with supporting rationale); (e) a tiered subscription model (free basic analysis, paid professional tier with detailed reports, dose optimization, multi-horizon mode, and chelator combination analysis); and (f) regular model updates as new training data, metals, and chelators are added. The intellectual property value is not in the ML algorithm (standard Gradient Boosting) but in the carefully designed training dataset encoding real geochemical relationships, the scientific framework connecting PHREEQC simulations to practical remediation decisions, and the validated methodology backed by a peer-reviewed publication. Before commercialization, Tier 3 bench validation (Goal 3) should be completed to ensure predictions are trustworthy for real-world use, and the methodology paper (Goal 8) should be published to establish scientific credibility. Partnership with one or two environmental consulting firms for beta testing on real projects would be extremely valuable for both validation and initial market development. The product name, branding, and legal structure (LLC, patent considerations for the methodology) would need to be addressed before commercial launch.

## GOAL 10: EXPANDING PARAMETERS AND ENVIRONMENTAL COMPLEXITY

This goal addresses the broader scientific limitations identified in the current model and pushes toward a more realistic representation of soil systems. Current simplifications that should be addressed include: (a) closed system vs. open system — real soils exchange gases with the atmosphere (CO2 affects carbonate chemistry, O2 affects redox), receive precipitation inputs, and lose water and solutes through drainage and plant uptake. PHREEQC can partially address this by specifying gas-phase equilibria (EQUILIBRIUM_PHASES with CO2(g) and O2(g)), and HP1 integration (Goal 4) would handle water flow explicitly. (b) Equilibrium vs. kinetics — many important soil reactions are slow: mineral dissolution/precipitation (hours to years), chelator degradation (EDTA half-life in soil is weeks to months depending on microbial activity), metal desorption from aged contamination (metals that have diffused into mineral lattice sites over decades may not be extractable even with strong chelators), and redox reactions. PHREEQC has a KINETICS block for rate-controlled reactions, and incorporating kinetic rate expressions from published literature for key reactions (chelator biodegradation, mineral dissolution, metal desorption from aged sites) would significantly improve realism. (c) Biological processes — microbial activity affects metal speciation through siderophore production, organic acid exudation, sulfide generation in anaerobic zones, and direct enzymatic reduction of metals. These cannot be modeled thermodynamically but their effects can be incorporated as empirical correction factors calibrated against bench experiments (Goal 3). (d) Temperature effects — current simulations assume 25 degrees C, but RI soils experience temperatures from below freezing to over 30 degrees C seasonally, which affects both thermodynamic equilibria and kinetic rates. Adding temperature as a parameter (5, 15, 25 degrees C representing winter, spring/fall, summer) would add another dimension to the training data. (e) Soil heterogeneity — real contaminated sites have spatial variability in all properties. The multi-horizon mode (Goal 7) addresses vertical variability, but lateral heterogeneity could be addressed by encouraging users to run the model at multiple sampling points and providing a site-level summary. (f) Competing organic contaminants — many contaminated sites have both metals and organic contaminants (PAHs, PCBs, petroleum), and surfactant-enhanced remediation for organics can interact with chelator-enhanced remediation for metals. This is a longer-term consideration that would require coupling with organic contaminant fate models.

## INTER-MODEL DISAGREEMENT STRATEGY

When integrating predictions from PHREEQC, ORCHESTRA, and HP1/HPx (Goal 4), the models may produce different predictions for the same input conditions because they use different thermodynamic databases, different approaches to surface complexation and organic matter binding, and different assumptions about equilibrium vs. kinetic control. The strategy for handling disagreement has four tiers. First, if all models agree within 5 percent for a given prediction, use the mean as a high-confidence consensus estimate. Second, if models disagree moderately (5 to 15 percent difference), use a weighted average where weights are determined by each model's validated accuracy against Tier 2 and Tier 3 experimental data — the model that best matches real-world observations gets the highest weight. Third, if models disagree substantially (greater than 15 percent difference), flag the prediction as uncertain in the interface, report the range of predictions rather than a single value, and indicate which model conditions (for example, high organic matter with strong chelators) tend to produce the most disagreement so practitioners know to exercise extra caution. Fourth, the ML model itself can be trained to learn which conditions produce high inter-model variance by including model-source as a feature and training an additional uncertainty estimation model (using quantile regression or prediction intervals from ensemble methods) that predicts not just the expected percent free metal but also the confidence interval around that prediction. This uncertainty quantification would be a major selling point for the commercial product because it tells practitioners not just "use EDTA at 150 mg/L" but "use EDTA at 150 mg/L, and we are 90 percent confident the free lead will be between 2 and 8 percent."
